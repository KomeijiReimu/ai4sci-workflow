# Independent review: what GDP growth adds to the longevity comparison

Recalculating from the original endpoints gives **1.37 more years of life-expectancy gain** for a 1.5-fold higher GDP per capita growth factor, comparing economies in the same region with similar starting income and longevity. The 95% interval, **0.45 to 2.29 years**, distinguishes a positive association from zero but not from the [six-month benchmark](plan.json). The comparison with curved baselines clears six months; the comparison ending in 2015 does not. Neither supersedes the primary estimate. When East Asia and Pacific is omitted, even the direction is uncertain.

How much does growth add? Starting conditions and region account for **41.8%** of the observed variation in longevity gains; including growth raises that share to **47.7%**. The difference is 5.9 percentage points of total variation, or 10.2% of the variation left after adjustment. The [paper](paper.md) uses the latter denominator correctly. These are descriptions of the economies studied, not a test of how accurately growth predicts health progress elsewhere.

The stronger association with HDI on matched economies does not overturn the case for tracking longevity directly. The [UNDP definition](../provenance/undp-note.md) includes both life expectancy and an income measure based on **GNI**, not the study’s GDP series. The comparison cannot separate the contributions of the index components. Nor does the earlier [US-state comparison](../provenance/scherbov-note.md) do so: it compares two development measures at one date, not changes in growth and longevity. The dashboard recommendation fits the evidence: growth marked a tendency in this period; it did not establish what caused the longevity gains or how large they were in any particular economy.
