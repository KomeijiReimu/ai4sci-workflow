# Can a daytime bicycle count stand in for a full day on the Williamsburg Bridge?

**Recommendation · 25 November 2026.** Test whether the year used to convert daytime bicycle counts into 24-hour counts changes the answer for the *same earlier days*. Use the Williamsburg Bridge in New York City. Compare factors learned in summer 2018 and summer 2019: between those summers, overnight L subway service across the East River was reduced. If even a few years' separation can distort the conversion, a historian joining manual and automatic counts needs to show that uncertainty.

[NYC DOT's historical table (notes 1, 4 and 5)](https://www.nyc.gov/html/dot/downloads/pdf/east-river-bridge-counts-24hrs-1980-2025.pdf) says it scaled 07:00–19:00 counts from 1980–2013 by factors between 1.25 and 1.59 derived from three years of later automatic data. Those are conversion factors, not bounds on historical error. The proposed test recreates the missing hours on days for which they were actually recorded. It tests the assumption behind DOT's procedure; actual night traffic on the old manual-count dates remains unknown.

### First test

1. Use one counter, one direction-combined series, and one unit: **bicycle passages per local calendar day**, not riders. Take dry Tuesdays through Thursdays, July 8–August 31, in 2014, 2015, 2018 and 2019. This avoids the Independence Day holiday and compares like parts of the summer. Use the historical DOT feed and the nearby Central Park rain gauge. The [field and access notes](evidence.md#data-and-access) identify the site, quality codes and date join. Only one site's summer intervals are needed.
2. Within each month and weekday, divide the **sum of full-day passages** by the **sum from 07:00 up to 19:00** in 2018. Do the same independently for 2019. Apply each set of six factors to the *identical* 2014 and 2015 daytime counts. Keep the rest of each earlier day's counts masked until both reconstructions are fixed. Compare each estimate with the recorded full-day total: for each test summer report signed percentage error in the sum, alongside the typical absolute daily error so that cancellations remain visible. Repeat the paired calculation with whole weeks resampled within each summer, including the calibration summers.
3. **What would distinguish the accounts?** If only total volume changed while the daily schedule held steady, both reconstructions should be close to the recorded totals. If late riding gained share in 2019, its multiplier should overstate the earlier totals more than the 2018 multiplier. The [MTA schedule](https://www.mta.info/document/8071), “Weeknights 10 PM to 5 AM,” puts the service reduction *outside* the manual count window. Check whether the excess factor actually comes from 20:00–05:00 rather than the remaining omitted hours. A discrepancy concentrated elsewhere would count against the transit-timing explanation. Even the predicted pattern would not identify former subway riders or isolate a subway effect.

### How the answer would be used

Treat **±5%** (one passage in twenty) as a proposed tolerance for comparing reported *levels*, not a city standard. If both reconstructions and their week-based uncertainty ranges stay inside it in both early summers, retain the simple conversion for **comparable summer averages**; do not extend that finding back to 1980. If factor choice pushes a reconstruction materially outside it, show a calibration-year sensitivity range wherever converted and directly measured levels meet. If both miss similarly, the change may predate 2018 or reflect a shared clock, sensor or sampling bias; the 2019 schedule would not explain that common miss. Wide intervals, poor coverage or sensitivity to interval timestamp conventions leave the test inconclusive. Historical years sampled on just one day have additional sampling error that a good multiplier cannot remove.

### Why not test the Brooklyn Bridge opening first?

The [initial shortlist](01-shortlist.md) favored induced cycling versus diversion. The nearest [published evaluation, Table 2](https://doi.org/10.25140/2411-5363-2025-3%2841%29-310-321), already uses the nearby Manhattan Bridge as its control. Riders switching from Manhattan to Brooklyn would increase that contrast twice: a gain on one side and a loss on the other. Summing both bridges fixes that arithmetic, but growth in their combined count could still reflect diversion from a third route or shifts in travel during pandemic recovery. [DOT itself](https://www.nyc.gov/html/dot/html/pr2024/bicycle-ridership-all-time-high.shtml) documents other bridge-access improvements and bike-share expansion. **No-go on attributing new trips from these counters alone.** Independent before/after evidence on riders' routes and previous modes, with a credible unaffected comparison, would reopen that question. For the measurement test, in contrast, the missing part of the target day can actually be revealed.
