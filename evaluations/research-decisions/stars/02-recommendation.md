# Does the eclipse move when only the detector clock moves?

**Recommendation · 25 November 2026.** Can Kepler’s half-hour sampling make one eclipse appear a minute early and the other a minute late, in a pattern that repeats as the orbital and detector clocks drift past each other? Recombine the *same* minute exposures twice, moving the start of each half-hour bin by 15 short exposures the second time. A feature that follows the bin edges is a reason to distrust the timing measurement. A feature that stays with the eclipses and is resolved in the original minute data is a reason to keep it for astrophysical follow-up, even if its period matches a known instrumental beat.

**Why ask this narrower question?** [Rappaport et al., §3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/1302.0563#S3.SS2) excluded candidate third-star signals when the timing and predicted beat periods matched. [Tran et al., §§2.2–3, 7](https://arxiv.org/html/1305.4639v2) found opposite shifts of the two minima consistent with starspots, but also identified cadence beats and smoothed on a five-day scale. A bare short-versus-long comparison would add little: [Conroy et al., §§2.4–2.5](https://arxiv.org/html/1306.0512v2#S2.SS4) already measured both, and [Marcadon & Prša, §§3.2, 4.1, 4.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.07694v2) compared both resolutions on independent TESS observations. Changing the bin origin instead holds the star, aperture, observing epoch and integration length fixed. The question is whether **a component** of measured variability belongs to the sampling grid, not whether spots exist or whether a third star is present.

## First test to propose

1. **Use two named systems and two months.** [KIC 6836140](https://keplerebs.villanova.edu/overview/?k=6836140) and [KIC 10861842](https://keplerebs.villanova.edu/overview/?k=10861842) have similar periods and two catalogued eclipses, simultaneous short/long observing modes in Kepler quarter 11, and distinct, resolvable predicted beat periods (about 3.7 and 4.9 days, respectively). These are predictions from catalog periods and the exposure spacing, not detected periodicities. Select them on those inputs, not on timing behavior. Use month 1 for the eclipse profiles, ephemeris refinement and tests with imposed timing shifts; reserve month 2 for the comparison. Keep the catalog’s primary/secondary identities fixed across both months. The six required files total less than 20 MB; [file names, fields and terms](03-evidence.md#data-that-can-decide-the-test) are public.
2. **Measure the same events on both grids.** Within each star, average 30 consecutive short-cadence fluxes at the actual long-cadence boundaries, then repeat with boundaries moved by 15 short cadences. Use actual timestamps, identical detrending, and only complete bins and eclipse pairs available on both grids; do not interpolate across gaps. Check the unshifted reconstruction against the *observed* long-cadence timings first. For a baseline, use Tran’s three-point parabola at each minimum on both coarse grids. Separately register the resolved ingress and egress against the month-1 short-cadence profile, and repeat the coarse fit with that profile integrated over each exposure. A flat-bottomed eclipse (flagged for KIC 10861842) may have a measurable center from its two slopes but no well-defined lowest point. If only the parabola responds, report an estimator failure.
3. **Look at the predicted beat, not the largest peak.** For each orbit form the differential timing residual, in seconds: half the primary observed-minus-calculated time minus half the secondary one. A common delay largely cancels. Fit its sine and cosine coefficients at the fundamental sampling beat, using the **same reference phase** for both grids and the resolved series. Calculate that phase from the predicted primary’s fractional position between actual flight-grid centers; a fixed 29.4-minute barycentric clock drifts. At the fundamental, moving the grid by half a bin reverses a sampling term but leaves a displacement tied to the same eclipse epochs in phase. The half-difference of the two coefficient vectors estimates the grid-sensitive part; the half-sum estimates the part that survives. Test each star separately; count neither shifted copies nor successive eclipses as independent stars.
4. **Check that the distinction is measurable.** In month 1 use a repeating, stationary profile sampled at the measured times as the sampling-only control. Then impose opposite sinusoidal shifts of 60 seconds on its two eclipses at the beat, before integration, and check recovery in the presence of realistic residual noise. A minute is a useful decision scale: smaller than the several-minute excursions in Tran’s illustrative systems but comparable to reported single-eclipse timing errors. Report joint 95% uncertainty regions for the sine and cosine coefficients, carrying shared exposure noise through the series together in contiguous blocks of orbits. Stop for either star if usable coverage falls below three beat cycles in either month, the two slopes cannot localize the center, the injected component cannot be distinguished from zero, or timings remeasured from original long-cadence flux disagree with the unshifted reconstruction at the one-minute scale.

## Decision from the held-out month

| Observation | Action |
|---|---|
| A component of at least 60 s reverses phase when the bins move, as in the stationary control; the minute data and the half-sum bound any surviving component below 60 s. | Flag this beat component as sampling-sensitive for this estimator and target. Do not turn its amplitude into a spot migration rate. |
| A component of at least 60 s survives with consistent phase in the minute data and the half-sum; simply moving the grid cannot account for it. | Retain it for light-curve shape or orbital analysis rather than automatically vetoing a beat match. If a reversing term is also resolved, report both components. |
| No component reaches the decision scale, or errors span both explanations. | Report an upper bound if supported, otherwise no decision. Do not enlarge the sample or tune the period in search of a detection. |

The resolved light curve is a reference for *photometric* motion, not the time of conjunction. Evolving asymmetry, pulsation, a blend, or orbital dynamics can survive either binning. Higher sampling harmonics need not reverse at this shift. These selected cases do not estimate a population fraction or adjudicate the 50–200-day variations studied by Tran et al.

**Why not start with the [rotation alternative](01-initial-choice.md)?** Its scientific payoff is a choice between two rotation periods for a weakly variable dwarf, but its independent clock is often too imprecise: [Lu et al., §3.2, Table 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2206.05411v2#S3.T1) place only 33 of 70 stars without reported companions in their higher-precision seismic group, *before* restricting to disputed periods. Even a precise splitting averages different stellar latitudes and depths from spots. Reopen the rotation question if a metadata-only crossmatch identifies a useful set of weak-peak stars with seismic precision sufficient to separate the two photometric estimates. The binary test already has contemporaneous inputs and an intervention with a predicted change of sign. **No timing or flux comparison has been run.**
