# Select a question

**Papers only:** start with the topic; no study setup is needed. Use scholarly search available in this session, check the identity at the publisher or original paper, and read the relevant passage. Give the title, stable link or DOI, finding, and why it matters to this question. Distinguish abstract-only access from full text; leave an inaccessible finding unverified. Stop at the annotated list.

**Ideas only:** for a publication-oriented recommendation, including one aimed at a venue or tier, follow the [publication workflow](../ai4sci/references/publication.md) through “Recommend a question.” Deliver the decision and any *proposed* first comparison, then stop before freezing a plan, running a pilot or drafting a paper. For a full publication commission, continue from that review into study design if the question survives. Work on benign scientific questions; exclude directions that would enable exploitation, biological misuse or physical harm.

## When a publication target matters

If the commission ends with a [remote experiment package](../ai4sci/references/remote-package.md), use the strong methods reviewer when choosing the question as well as when settling its design. A literature search on its own still ends at the reading list.

If the author specifies only a band (e.g. CCF B) and the field is clear, choose a relevant venue and track as a working target. Cite the venue's entry in the applicable ranking directory and its edition, and verify the track in the venue's own program. Explain the choice rather than waiting for the author to name a venue.

For fit, inspect actual recent accepted papers in that track. Give the years or date range searched. For each comparison that bears on the decision, cite the official program or proceedings entry and the exact section, page or table in the paper. Compare the contribution and the evidence behind it with what the proposed study could deliver. Select papers for relevance, not to meet a quota. Use this sample to judge fit; search separately for work that could already answer the question.

## From a broad topic to competing questions

When the author gives a topic but no question, start with things that need explaining, not a list of paper titles to write. One investigator can look for a disagreement between two original results or a conclusion that depends on a neglected control. Another can start from a practical decision, a measurement that has become available, or an observation a common explanation gets wrong. Identify what each trigger comes from and who would use the answer. Do not turn someone's supplied question into an agent-originated idea. A specific, feasible question from the user needs a strong rival, not a tournament.

1. **Get two kinds of evidence before ranking.** Ask one reader to find the closest result and contrary evidence, and another to inspect what can actually be measured. Give each the topic and constraints, not a favored answer; ask for source or data locators. Use separate readers only if the host provides them. Working alone, do both passes and call them self-checked. Two readers citing the same experiment do not supply independent empirical support.
2. **Search for the claim, not just its name.** Search beyond the target venue, rank and recent editions for prior work that could anticipate the claim. Try synonyms from adjacent fields and search on the population, outcome or baseline. Follow the references of the closest paper. For each serious candidate cite the nearest original result (version and section, table or page); state what overlaps and what remains unanswered. Merge candidates that would run the same comparison and support the same inference. If a paper already answers the question, cite the passage and drop the novelty claim, or explain the value of a reproduction. Report the scope of the search; supplied material alone supports only a local screen.
3. **Check whether the inputs can decide it.** Inspect a versioned dataset description, schema or permitted development sample. Identify the unit, target, available-at-prediction-time variables and group/time keys needed for the split; check access, permitted use and cost. For analytic or simulation work, identify the actual reference or controllable input instead. Say what you inspected. If a missing label, comparison group or dependency prevents the test, mark the candidate conditional rather than calling the dataset ready. Leave held-out outcomes unopened.
4. **Choose between genuinely different explanations.** For an open topic, compare a small set of candidates; for a supplied idea, challenge it with its strongest plausible rival. For each finalist, give a prediction from each explanation under the *same* proposed contrast, the baseline, and an observation that would weaken the favored account. If both predict the same result at attainable precision, change the contrast or defer the question. A new model name or another parameter setting is not by itself a different explanation.
5. **Let a challenge change the shortlist.** For a publication recommendation, send the front-runner, strongest alternative, proposed comparison and evidence to a strong scientific reviewer independent of the proposal. Use the returned objection to decide; have the reviewer check a replacement before recommending it. If no suitable reviewer or substitute can run, make a separate counterevidence pass and label the decision self-checked, independent review pending. For other ideas, use an independent reader or do the pass yourself. Test the front-runner against a prior result, confounder, missing comparison or measurement limit, with a source or data locator. Distinguish a claim already answered elsewhere from an open question whose evidence would not serve the track.
6. **Redraft or retire.** If the objection stands, record the original question, what failed and the replacement: what unit, mechanism, contrast or decision is now different? Recheck the changed claim against prior work and the target, then compare it with the best surviving alternative. Renaming a predictor or adding a dataset without changing the test is not a new candidate. On resuming a study, consult its rejected questions and their reasons. Reopen one only if new data or evidence changes that reason. No new round is required if the first choice survives a substantive challenge. If every candidate fails and another genuinely different starting point is affordable, use it. Otherwise deliver a no-go and name the missing observation, rather than promising a study that cannot discriminate.
7. **Recommend one next decision.** Recommend proceeding, changing the question or target, or retiring it. Tie that decision to the closest prior result and, when relevant, the track comparison. For a survivor give the feasible first comparison, opposing predictions, interpretation under either outcome, and why it beats the strongest alternative. Name the specific evidence that would reverse the choice. In an ongoing study save that decision and changed or rejected candidates where the handoff can point to them. For a one-off request give the answer directly.

## When the requested product is a review

Retain the search scope and inclusion criteria. For a systematic review, record databases, dates, exact searches, screening counts and reasons for excluding near matches. Deduplicate versions; preserve genuine disagreements about methods, populations or outcomes. Check corrections and retractions for decisive sources. Save permitted excerpts with locators, not entire copyrighted works for redistribution.

For a **formal audited** source-based review, follow the checkout's [file contract](../../checks/contract.md): freeze the review question and criteria with `require_source_manifest: true`; register the retained snapshots in `research/sources.json`. Record `status: "not-applicable"` and the reason in `research/run.json`. Use literal quote evidence, with `displays` for source-reported numbers. These records are unnecessary for a reading list or idea shortlist.

The screening decisions adapt [ARIS candidate selection](https://github.com/wanshuiyin/Auto-claude-code-research-in-sleep/blob/4ba94ae2a1c1fbbb14858750dd8764cabf6c28bd/skills/idea-creator/SKILL.md) and its [claim-level novelty screen](https://github.com/wanshuiyin/Auto-claude-code-research-in-sleep/blob/4ba94ae2a1c1fbbb14858750dd8764cabf6c28bd/skills/novelty-check/SKILL.md); the feasibility pass draws on [EvoScientist's data and protocol research brief](https://github.com/EvoScientist/EvoScientist/blob/850df6f075505d74b69e34933b4d0c989422b094/EvoScientist/subagents/research.yaml). These are design sources, not evidence for the user's topic.
